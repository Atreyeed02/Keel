01The accounting equation

Everything a business owns was paid for by someone: by lenders, or by its owners. So what it owns always equals what it owes plus what its owners have put in:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

While the business trades, revenue adds to the owners' stake and expenses take from it, so until the year's books are closed the equation reads:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity + (Revenue − Expenses)

Worked example: four of the consultancy's transactions, in USD

02Debits and credits

Every transaction is written as entries, and every entry is either a debit or a credit. On paper, debits go in an account's left column and credits in its right. The words come from Latin (debit from debere, "to owe"; credit from credere, "to entrust"), but today they name only the side. Neither means good or bad, and neither always means increase or decrease. What an entry does depends on the account's type:

  • {{ ui.side("debit", hidden=True) }}A debit increases assets and expenses, and decreases liabilities, equity and revenue.
  • {{ ui.side("credit", hidden=True) }}A credit increases liabilities, equity and revenue, and decreases assets and expenses.

Keel shows debits in blue and credits in amber, always labelled Dr and Cr too. It never uses red and green, because a credit isn't a loss.

03Normal side and T-accounts

An account's normal side is the side that increases it: debit for assets and expenses, credit for liabilities, equity and revenue. Keel shows every balance on its account's normal side, so an account that has grown shows a positive number. A balance below zero means more went out on the other side than came in, such as cash overdrawn.

A T-account is the paper bookkeeper's sketch of one account: debits under the left arm, credits under the right. Luca Pacioli described double-entry bookkeeping in print in 1494.

05DEAD CLIC

D·E·A·D

Debits increase Expenses, Assets and Drawings.

C·L·I·C

Credits increase Liabilities, Income and Capital.

Income is revenue and capital is equity. Drawings (money the owner takes out) has no type of its own in Keel: record it as a debit to an equity account, which reduces the owner's stake.

06When a transaction balances

A transaction is balanced when its debits equal its credits. Keel accepts one only when:

  1. it has at least two entries;
  2. every amount is more than zero, with at most two decimal places;
  3. in each currency, debits equal credits;
  4. every entry names an existing account, in that account's currency.

If any rule fails, nothing is written and the form says which rule. The database checks the balance again when the transaction commits, so even a write that bypassed the app couldn't leave the books lopsided. One transaction may hold several currencies if each balances by itself, but it can't convert between them: converting currency takes an exchange account, which Keel doesn't have yet.

A traditional trial balance lists every account's balance in debit and credit columns. Keel's overview adds up every debit and credit entry per currency instead. Either way, when every transaction balances, the two totals are equal.

07How Keel records it

Every account opened and every transaction posted is also written as an event, in the same database transaction; the event log lists them. The database refuses to change or delete an event, and the ledger's tables can be rebuilt from the events in order. So nothing is edited: to fix a mistake, post a transaction with the same entries on the opposite sides, then post the right one. On the public demo, the nightly reset is the one exception: it empties the ledger and the log together.
